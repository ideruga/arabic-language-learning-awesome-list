\subsection{\textarabic{لِمَ} — [lima]}
\begin{tabular}{p{3cm}p{10cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Translation} & why / for what reason \\
\textbf{Root} & \textarabic{م-ا} (interrogative particle) \\
\textbf{Pattern} & Combination of \textarabic{لِ} (li-) preposition + \textarabic{مَا} (maË) interrogative \\
\textbf{Grammar} & Interrogative particle meaning ``why'', formed from preposition \textarabic{لِ} (for) + \textarabic{مَا} (what) \\
\midrule
\textbf{Examples} & \makecell[l]{\parbox{9.5cm}{

\midrule
\textbf{Synonyms} & \textarabic{لِمَاذَا} (limaaðaː), \textarabic{لِأَيِّ شَيْءٍ} (for what thing) \\
\textbf{Etymology} & Contraction of \textarabic{لِ} (for) + \textarabic{مَا} (what), common in Classical and Modern Standard Arabic \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\vspace{1em}
\textbf{Usage Notes:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textarabic{لِمَ} is more formal and classical than \textarabic{لِمَاذَا}
\item The final \textarabic{ا} is dropped from \textarabic{مَا} when combined with \textarabic{لِ}
\item Often used in rhetorical questions expressing frustration or disbelief
\end{itemize}

\section{Phrase Analysis}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=boxcolor,colframe=headercolor,breakable]
\textbf{Grammatical Structure:}\\
Interrogative particle + passive imperfect verb (1st person) + prepositional phrase (location) + object noun (accusative) + relative descriptive clause (negation + noun + prepositional phrase) + prepositional phrase (scope)

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Syntactic Breakdown:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{\textarabic{لِمَ}} — Interrogative (why)
\item \textbf{\textarabic{أُقْحَمُ}} — Main verb (passive imperfect, 1st person singular)
\item \textbf{\textarabic{فِي مُخِّي}} — Prepositional phrase indicating location (into my brain)
\item \textbf{\textarabic{لُغَةً}} — Direct object (a language, accusative indefinite)
\item \textbf{\textarabic{لَا طَائِلَةَ مِنْهَا}} — Relative descriptive clause modifying \textarabic{لُغَة}
\item \textbf{\textarabic{فِي الدُّنْيَا}} — Prepositional phrase indicating scope (in the world)
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Key Grammar Points:}
\begin{itemize}
\item The passive voice \textarabic{أُقْحَمُ} implies external force or compulsion without naming the agent
\item \textarabic{لِمَ} is a contracted form more literary than \textarabic{لِمَاذَا}, adding rhetorical weight
\item The indefinite accusative \textarabic{لُغَةً} with tanwÄ«n indicates any/a language (non-specific)
\item \textarabic{لَا طَائِلَةَ مِنْهَا} is a classical idiom meaning "no benefit/use from it"
\item The prepositional phrase \textarabic{مِنْهَا} creates a genitive relationship (benefit FROM it)
\item \textarabic{فِي الدُّنْيَا} emphasizes worldly/practical utility as the criterion
\item The rhetorical question structure implies criticism of imposed language learning
\item The metaphor \textarabic{فِي مُخِّي} (into my brain) suggests forced mental effort
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Additional Learning Notes}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=boxcolor,colframe=accentcolor,title=\textbf{Cultural and Literary Context},breakable]
\textbf{Educational Critique:} This phrase reflects a common sentiment among Arabic-speaking students frustrated with mandatory curricula that seem disconnected from practical life. The use of classical Arabic itself adds irony — complaining about useless language learning in formal literary Arabic.

\textbf{Rhetorical Force:} The passive voice \textarabic{أُقْحَمُ} removes agency from the speaker, suggesting systemic imposition rather than personal choice. This grammatical choice strengthens the complaint by emphasizing powerlessness.

\textbf{Classical Idiom:} \textarabic{لَا طَائِلَةَ مِنْهُ} is a sophisticated classical expression rarely used in modern colloquial speech, appearing primarily in formal writing and educated discourse. Its use here demonstrates high linguistic competence even while questioning language utility.

\textbf{Worldly vs. Sacred:} The term \textarabic{الدُّنْيَا} specifically means "the worldly life" as opposed to \textarabic{الْآخِرَة} (the hereafter), suggesting the speaker values practical, material utility over abstract or spiritual knowledge.

\textbf{Brain Metaphor:} Using \textarabic{مُخّ} (brain/marrow) rather than \textarabic{عَقْل} (mind/intellect) emphasizes the physical, forced nature of memorization — cramming into the brain rather than understanding with the mind.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Memory Tips}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Interrogative Pattern:} \textarabic{لِمَ} = \textarabic{لِ} (for) + \textarabic{مَا} (what) → "for what?" = "why?"
\item \textbf{Passive Recognition:} \textarabic{أُقْحَمُ} has \textarabic{أُ} prefix (1st person passive marker) + \textarabic{ـُ} ending (indicative mood)
\item \textbf{Form IV Pattern:} \textarabic{أَقْحَمَ} follows \textarabic{أَفْعَلَ} pattern — glottal stop + doubled middle root letter
\item \textbf{Classical Idiom Chain:} \textarabic{لَا} + noun (accusative) + \textarabic{مِنْ} = "no [noun] from..."
\item \textbf{Tanwīn Clue:} \textarabic{لُغَةً} with \textarabic{ـً} shows accusative case (direct object)
\item \textbf{Pronoun Reference:} \textarabic{مِنْهَا} refers back to \textarabic{لُغَة} (both feminine)
\item \textbf{Worldly Focus:} \textarabic{الدُّنْيَا} always means material world (vs. \textarabic{الْآخِرَة} spiritual realm)
\end{enumerate}

\section{Advanced Grammar Notes}

\subsection{Passive Voice Formation in Form IV}

The verb \textarabic{أُقْحَمُ} demonstrates passive voice in Form IV:

\textbf{Active Form IV:} \textarabic{أَقْحَمَ} [Ê"aqĥama] — he forced/crammed\\
\textbf{Pattern:} \textarabic{أَفْعَلَ} [Ê"afÊ•ala]

\textbf{Passive Form IV:} \textarabic{أُقْحِمَ} [Ê"uqĥima] — he was forced/crammed\\
\textbf{Pattern:} \textarabic{أُفْعِلَ} [Ê"ufÊ•ila]

\textbf{Passive Imperfect:} \textarabic{يُقْحَمُ} [juqĥamu] — he is being forced\\
\textbf{Pattern:} \textarabic{يُفْعَلُ} [jufÊ•alu]

\textbf{Key Changes:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Perfect: \textarabic{أَ} → \textarabic{أُ} (1st vowel: a → u)
\item Perfect: \textarabic{ـَـ} → \textarabic{ـِـ} (2nd vowel: a → i)
\item Imperfect: \textarabic{يَ} → \textarabic{يُ} (prefix vowel: a → u)
\item Imperfect: middle vowel deleted, final \textarabic{ـُ} added
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Idiom \textarabic{لَا ... مِنْ}}

This classical construction expresses categorical negation:

\textbf{Structure:} \textarabic{لَا} + noun (accusative) + \textarabic{مِنْ} + pronoun/noun

\textbf{Examples:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textarabic{لَا بُدَّ مِنْهُ} — It is necessary (lit: no escape from it)
\item \textarabic{لَا خَيْرَ فِيهِ} — There is no good in it
\item \textarabic{لَا شَكَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ} — There is no doubt in that
\item \textarabic{لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ} — There is no uncertainty in it
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Accusative of Specification}

\textarabic{لُغَةً} appears in accusative case as the direct object of the passive verb. In passive constructions, what was the active object becomes the passive subject, but here it remains accusative, serving as a specification:

\textbf{Active (hypothetical):} *They force me a language*\\
\textbf{Passive:} \textarabic{أُقْحَمُ لُغَةً} — I am forced [regarding] a language

This is a nuance of Form IV passive verbs that can retain accusative objects.
